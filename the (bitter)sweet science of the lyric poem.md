I remember a few years back, the Dalai Lama made a comment along the lines of "Buddhism is a subjective science". This immediately struck me as a bit of an oxymoron in the sense that the third person methodology is an inherent part of science and a core sticking point over whether there can be a science of consciousness. 

That isn't to say that Buddhism and other forms of self enquiry aren't methodical. In the same way in which technique can play a part in everything from sport to public speech, subjectivity itself can be a precise and exacting practice. It's not that contemplative methods don't necessarily yield results, it's more that the subject is the object of enquiry and is also the only one to be able to observe the results. 

This practice doesn't have to be something strictly ascetic or subject to rigorous concentration. It could simply be the act of observing the effect a song might have on one's mood or listening out for a sudden, intrusive commentary that often murmurs throughout our days while simultaneously avoiding observation and interrogation. 

Similarly, it is easy to dismiss a lyric poem (a short poem written in the first person where the speaker is identified with the writer and focuses on their subjective experience) as navel gazing. But, as the Romantic odes often show us, the lyric poem can be an exact and forensic act of self enquiry. While each stanza of Ode to a Nightingale might seem to portray whose mood seems to shift at the slightest whim, they also portray an exact and tangible view of a part of Keats's experience before it shifts again with the next stanza. 

There's also something to be said for political lyric poetry in which the poet doesn't set up the *lyric I* as a pure moral centre point from which the corruption of the world is observed and judged. While I wouldn't consider myself to be a committed Jungian, I've often felt that many of these poetic moral paragons could practice a bit more shadow work.

I think this is one reason why I keep returning to the poetry of Adrian C Louis. Describing himself as an "Indian half-breed", Adrian lived in Minnesota and died a few years ago. He was probably my favourite living poet in the years prior. His poetry was personal, political, spiritual and profane. And while I could go to pretty much any of his poems to show how he was able to investigate how his own character could be corrupted by the greater political milieux, I'll instead quote a short that I felt called me out during one of own previous holier than thou phases of my career:

### Meta-Metamorphosis

Once upon a time there was a gecko.
When he looked into the mirror,
he saw Godzilla. Pleased with the power
of his imagination, the gecko decided
to become a writer. He had a profound
ability to spew luxurious words.
There seemed to be a direct vein
from his brain to his fingertips.
Great! Except it bypassed his heart. 
Shit transpired. Shit backfired.
Long story short: that rock in yonder field?
Those sweating, haunted eyes peering out?
It is a toad who was once a gecko.

*Adrian C Louis, f⁠rom Random Exorcisms (2016)*

This isn't necessarily one of those lyric poems that I've been waxing lyrical about a few paragraphs back but it did serve as a word of warning that I took very seriously, even though I still might have found myself entering the early stages of my my sweaty-eyed toad phase. 

At the time I read it I felt that the poem had me bang to rights. I was bashing out a prose poem every other day, often taking aim at the assortment of ghouls of the 2016 shite-geist. I was probably in the throes of my Twitter addiction too and so my politicised superego was calling the creative shots. Then I read this poem on Adrian's Facebook page and immediately saw myself in the gecko. We sometimes exchanged comments and likes on each others pages and I genuinely suspected that the poem was about me. 

In the years since then (Adrian died in 2018) I've not been publishing as many poems but I've got better at looking within. While the gecko in Adrian's poem is able to string a vein between his brain and fingertips, I found that you can't really rig up the same kind of line from the heart. Sometimes the heart has no words to offer much like the flames in Milton's version of hell shed no light.
